SemLiFi: Burst-Aware Semantic Recovery With
Confidence-Gated Fallback for LiFi Links
Dr. Chirra Venkata Ramireddy, Abhay Sharma, Mahi Bhardwaj, Abhishek Choudhary, and Kumari Sandhya
Abstract—Visible light communication (VLC) and Light Fidelity
(LiFi) links are highly sensitive to temporary line-of-sight
blockage. Unlike independent bit errors, a physical obstruction
can remove a contiguous interval of symbols, producing a
burst loss whose duration and location depend on obstruction
dynamics and link geometry. This paper presents SemLiFi, a
cross-layer burst-recovery framework that combines a physically
implemented LiFi testbed, controlled optical occlusion, empirical
burst characterization, Burst-Aware Semantic Reconstruction
(BASR), and a Confidence-Gated Fallback Protocol (CGFP). BASR
reconstructs missing structured telemetry from surviving context
and burst information, while CGFP routes each reconstruction
either to semantic acceptance or to selective exact recovery.
The prototype uses ESP32 transmit and receive nodes, on–
off keying with Manchester coding, a high-intensity LED, a
BPW34 photodiode, an LM358 transimpedance amplifier, CRC-8
validation, and an SG90 servo-controlled optical flap. The reported
study contains 56 empirical occlusion events, a 74,281-parameter
edge-constrained Transformer BASR model, and disjoint 664/641-
burst calibration/test splits for CGFP. The clean-link path reports
449.0 ms mean latency with zero bit errors. The evaluated Reed–
Solomon configuration recovers 37.5% of observed occlusion
bursts, while Stop-and-Wait ARQ reports 97.1% delivery at 1560.0
ms mean latency. BASR reports 42.63±1.47% exact reconstruction
accuracy and 100.0% accuracy on the evaluated motor-state
field. At the calibrated threshold τ ∗ = 0.80, accepted semantic
patches have 97.13% precision and a 0.78% patch-error rate, with
27.15% of retransmissions avoided in the reported simulation.
Continuous-burst simulation reaches 100% delivery at 790.7
ms with 1.00 ±0.13% UFER, while a 15-frame live run reaches
100% delivery and 0.00% UFER by rejecting all low-confidence
semantic reconstructions and invoking exact fallback. The results
support the feasibility of the integrated recovery architecture
within the tested structured-telemetry and controlled-occlusion
setting, while the small live-hardware sample and absence of
completed component ablations limit broader claims.
Index Terms—LiFi, visible light communication, optical wireless
communication, burst errors, semantic communication, semantic
reconstruction, confidence gating, edge inference, embedded
Transformer, error-resilient telemetry.

The same line-of-sight dependence that is useful for optical
spatial confinement creates a distinctive reliability problem. A
person crossing an optical path, a temporary object, equipment
movement, receiver rotation, or another obstruction can atten-
uate the received signal for a finite interval. Consequently,
errors need not be independent in time. Instead, several
adjacent symbols or bytes can become unavailable together.
For a physical system, the resulting burst length is linked to
obstruction duration, optical geometry, modulation timing, and
receiver thresholding. A recovery mechanism that assumes only
independent random errors can therefore fail to represent the
dominant impairment mode of an obstructed optical link.
Conventional error-control mechanisms remain essential in
this setting. Forward error correction (FEC) adds redundancy
so that a receiver can recover corrupted information without
a retransmission. Reed–Solomon (RS) codes are a classical
family of block codes with well-established symbol-error
and burst-error decoding properties [5], [6]. Their correction
capability, however, is bounded by the redundancy selected
for the code. Automatic repeat request (ARQ) instead requests
missing or corrupted information again and can preserve exact
payload content, but the retransmission itself consumes time
and requires a return path. When the forward optical path is
temporarily unavailable, retransmission latency can become
significant.
Semantic communication offers a complementary perspective.
Rather than treating every source symbol as equally important,
semantic systems exploit source structure, context, or task
objectives to infer information that is useful to the receiver
[7]–[9]. Recent work has also demonstrated semantic commu-
nication over visible-light links and underwater optical wireless
channels, showing that learning-based semantic methods can
be coupled to physical optical communication systems [10],
[11]. These developments motivate a narrower question: can
contextual semantic reconstruction be used specifically for
temporary optical burst loss while preserving an exact-recovery
path whenever the reconstruction is not sufficiently trustworthy?
This paper presents SemLiFi, a cross-layer architecture
designed around that question. SemLiFi does not claim that
semantic reconstruction should replace FEC or ARQ. Instead,

II. RELATEDWORK ANDRESEARCHGAP
A. Optical Wireless Communication
The physical behavior of VLC links has been extensively
studied. Komine and Nakagawa developed a foundational
analysis of indoor VLC links, including optical channel gain
and geometric factors [1]. Elgalaet al.surveyed indoor
optical wireless communication and highlighted practical issues
associated with optical front ends, modulation, and link design
[2]. Pathaket al.subsequently reviewed VLC communication,
networking, and sensing, identifying challenges spanning the
physical, networking, and application layers [3]. These studies
establish the physical context in which temporary line-of-sight
blockage can be treated as a channel impairment.
The present work focuses on a narrower failure mode:
a temporary physical obstruction that creates a localized,
contiguous loss interval. The objective is not to develop a new
optical propagation model. Instead, the measured burst behavior
is used as an input to the recovery layer. This distinction is
important because the burst distribution becomes part of the
semantic recovery problem rather than merely a post hoc stress
test.
B. FEC and ARQ
Reed–Solomon coding was introduced as a finite-field
polynomial code and has subsequently been used for reliable
communication systems requiring symbol-error correction [5].
Burst-error decoding methods further demonstrate how code
structure can be exploited when errors occur in clusters [6]. In
SemLiFi, RS-FEC is retained as an exact-recovery baseline. The
evaluation uses RS(33,25), for which the selected configuration
hast= 4symbol-error correction capability.
ARQ provides a different exact-recovery mechanism. A
receiver detects an invalid frame and requests retransmission.
The approach is conceptually simple and remains appropriate
when exact source reproduction is mandatory. The drawback
considered in this work is recovery delay during a temporary
optical outage. SemLiFi therefore treats ARQ as the fallback
path rather than as a mechanism to be eliminated.
C. Semantic Communication
Deep-learning semantic communication has explored the
use of learned representations to communicate information
relevant to a receiver’s task rather than enforcing bit-perfect
reconstruction in every setting [7]. Qinet al.discuss semantic
communication principles and challenges, while G ¨und¨uzet
al.survey context-, semantic-, and task-oriented communi-
cation architectures [8], [9]. These works motivate the use
of contextual information but do not specifically address the
combination of a physically measured optical burst, a localized
structured-payload reconstruction task, and an exact fallback
gate.
Maet al.recently demonstrated a visible-light semantic
communication system with a physical VLC platform and
investigated semantic throughput under practical VLC con-
straints [10]. Nennoucheet al.investigated semantic underwater
optical wireless communication using a variational autoencoder
and evaluated robustness against underwater optical channel
impairments [11]. SemLiFi addresses a different recovery
objective: it focuses on temporary localized burst loss and
on deciding when a reconstructed telemetry segment should
be trusted.
D. Research Gap
The research gap considered here is the intersection of three
requirements:
1) the impairment is generated by a physical optical obstruc-
tion;
2) semantic reconstruction is explicitly conditioned on the
resulting burst characteristics; and
3) semantic output is not trusted unconditionally but is passed
through a calibrated confidence gate with an exact-recovery
fallback.
The contribution is therefore architectural and experimental
rather than a claim that VLC, semantic communication, or FEC
is individually new. The evaluation also separates simulated

C. Frame and Recovery Semantics
The implemented frame uses the structure
[0xAA 0x55][LEN][SEQ][PAYLOAD][CRC8][\r\n].
The synchronization word is two bytes. The length field
identifies the payload length and the sequence identifier
supports selective retransmission. The payload is a structured
telemetry string. CRC-8 uses the Dallas/Maxim polynomial
x8 +x 5 +x 4 + 1 (0x31). CRC validation is used for exact
bit-level integrity; it is not treated as evidence that a semantic
reconstruction is correct.
IV. PHYSICALTESTBED ANDBURSTCHARACTERIZATION
A. Hardware Configuration
The transmitter contains an ESP32 microcontroller, a high-
intensity LED, and a transistor-based LED driver. The source
implementation identifies GPIO 23 as the LED control output
and uses on–off keying (OOK) with Manchester coding. The
receiver uses a BPW34 silicon PIN photodiode connected to
an LM358-based transimpedance amplifier (TIA), followed
by an ESP32 ADC input identified as GPIO 34. A software
OOK slicer and Manchester decoder recover the frame. An
SG90 servo-controlled flap is positioned between the optical
transmitter and receiver to create repeatable obstruction events.
The component choices are intended to make the testbed low-
cost and inspectable. The ESP32 provides the GPIO, ADC,
timing, and processing resources needed by the prototype.
The BPW34 provides a simple optical detector, while the
LM358 TIA converts the photodiode current into a measurable
voltage. The servo flap allows the duration and repetition of
physical blockage to be controlled more consistently than an
uncontrolled hand obstruction.
B. Optical Modulation and Sampling
The reported implementation uses a 1000 µs bit period,
corresponding to a raw rate of 1000 bps. Receiver sampling is
performed within a mid-bit window of approximately 450–550
µs into the bit interval. The mid-bit strategy is intended to
reduce sensitivity to transition transients and timing uncertainty.

C. Receiver Calibration
The reported receiver calibration measured dark ADC
values of approximately 0–5 counts and illuminated values
of approximately 95–176 counts. An operating threshold of
approximately 50 ADC counts was used. Manchester transition
consistency checks complemented CRC validation. These
measurements establish a substantial separation between the
observed dark and illuminated levels under the reported test
conditions, but the calibration is not assumed to remain constant
under arbitrary ambient illumination or geometry.
D. Burst Profiles
A burst is represented as a contiguous interval in which the
optical path becomes unavailable or unreliable. The logger
records start time, end time, duration, corrupted symbols,
affected bytes, frame identifier, and local signal information.
The study reports 56 empirical occlusion events across three
servo-driven profiles: P1 short blockage of approximately 15–
40 ms, P2 long blockage of approximately 150–380 ms, and
P3 mixed/randomized duration.
The empirical burst distribution is important for BASR.
Instead of masking independent characters with arbitrary
probabilities, the model is exposed to contiguous losses whose
duration is related to the physical obstruction process. The
source reports that physical obstructions corrupted approxi-
mately 10–25 contiguous bytes, which is substantially larger
than the four-symbol correction capability of the selected
RS(33,25) configuration in the evaluated cases.

E. Physical Failure Modes and Threat Model
The tested impairment is non-adversarial, physically gener-
ated burst loss. The system does not claim protection against
intentional frame forgery, telemetry spoofing, or an attacker
who injects syntactically valid false data. Three physical cases
remain particularly important: bursts longer than the training
distribution, repeated or overlapping obstructions outside the
measured profiles, and timing ambiguity near a CRC boundary.
These cases are relevant because the confidence model is
calibrated to the observed channel rather than to an arbitrary
worst-case distribution.
V. BURST-AWARESEMANTICRECONSTRUCTION
A. Structured Payload Assumption
BASR is intended for telemetry in which fields have syntax
and contextual regularities. Examples used in the study include
•TEMP=27.4,HUM=61,MOTOR=ON,
•DEVICE=04,TEMP=26.8,STATUS=ACTIVE, and
•VOLT=3.72,CURR=0.42,STATE=OK.
The structured format is central to the method. A receiver
can exploit field names, delimiters, neighboring values, and
application constraints to generate a candidate reconstruction.
This does not imply that arbitrary encrypted binary payloads
can be reconstructed. The method is evaluated only on the
structured telemetry setting described by the source.

B. Edge-Constrained Transformer
BASR is implemented as an edge-constrained Transformer
encoder with model dimension dmodel = 64 , four attention
heads, two encoder layers, and feed-forward dimension dff =
96. The reported model contains 74,281 trainable parameters,
against a 75,000-parameter budget. This corresponds to 99.04%
utilization of the stated budget. The architecture is deliberately
compact; the goal is not to maximize language-modeling
capacity but to provide a small contextual predictor that can
be coupled to an embedded communication receiver.
For a candidate missing sequence ˆYm, exact reconstruction
accuracy is defined as the fraction of evaluated missing
segments that exactly match the transmitted segment. Task or
field accuracy is computed separately for application-relevant
fields. This distinction is important because a semantic receiver
can preserve a task-critical state while failing to reproduce
every character of the original message.
C. Semantic Failure Example
Consider a payload containing
TEMP=27.4,HUM=61,MOTOR=ON.
A reconstruction such as
TEMP=21.4,HUM=61,MOTOR=ON
may remain syntactically valid while being semantically wrong.
A CRC cannot certify such a reconstruction because the CRC
is calculated over the original transmitted bits. Consequently,
semantic plausibility and exact correctness must be separated
in the recovery decision.

B. Burst-Severity Penalty
The physical burst information is incorporated through a
nonlinear nonincreasing penalty. The source records the penalty
parameters as α= 0.50 andγ= 1.15 . The purpose of the term
is qualitative and operational: as the fraction or span of missing
content increases, confidence in a contextual reconstruction
should not remain unchanged. The exact calibration is tied to
the studied payloads and measured burst distribution.
C. Selective Exact Fallback
When C < τ∗, the receiver issues a selective negative
acknowledgment (NACK) and requests the missing segment or
affected frame through the exact-recovery path. The recovered
content replaces the semantic candidate. This design means
that semantic inference is an optimization opportunity rather
than a mandatory reliability mechanism.
D. Illustrative Decision
For an illustrative frame with Ctoken = 0.93, Cphys = 0.90,
Penaltyburst = 0.95, and valid syntax, the resulting score is
C= 0.93×0.90×0.95≈0.795.(12)
Since this value is below τ∗ = 0.80 , CGFP rejects the
semantic candidate and invokes exact fallback. The example
demonstrates the conservative operating point: a candidate can
appear strong while still being rejected when its combined
confidence is marginal. If a candidate violates a hard structural
rule,S syntax = 0and fallback is immediate.

VIII. ANALYTICALINTERPRETATION OFBURSTRECOVERY
A. Burst Length and Coding Capability
The physical impairment and the coding configuration can
be compared at the symbol level. For a bit period Tb, a burst
of duration TB affects approximately NB≈T B/Tb bits before
accounting for frame boundaries, Manchester expansion, and
the distinction between bits and coded symbols. With the
reportedTb = 1000µ s, even the shortest P1 obstruction interval
of approximately 15 ms can affect a substantial contiguous
region of the transmitted stream. The long P2 intervals can
affect a much larger region. The exact number of corrupted
bytes depends on where the obstruction begins and ends relative
to the frame and on the Manchester decoding boundary.
The selected RS(33,25) configuration has 8 parity symbols
and a nominal correction capability of t= 4 symbol errors
under the evaluated decoding configuration. The physical
experiment reports approximately 10–25 contiguous corrupted
bytes during obstruction. This does not mean that every event
necessarily produces exactly 10–25 uncorrectable RS symbols,
because frame boundaries, symbol definitions, and the decoder’s
erasure/error handling affect the effective correction outcome.
It does, however, explain why a finite correction window can be
exceeded by the measured physical impairment. The reported
37.5% recovery rate is therefore interpreted as an empirical
result for the implemented configuration, not as a theoretical
claim that RS codes cannot handle optical burst errors.
B. Why the Semantic Path Can Reduce Retransmission
Suppose an affected frame contains a structured payload in
which some fields are predictable from surviving context. An
exact recovery system treats the missing region as unavailable
information and requests it again. A semantic recovery system
instead computes a candidate from Y−, Y +, and B. If the
candidate passes the CGFP gate, the receiver can deliver the
candidate without using the exact retransmission path. The
theoretical retransmission-saving fraction for a set of N frames
can be written as
Rsave = 1− M
N,(19)
where M is the number of frames that still require exact
recovery. The source reports 27.15% retransmissions avoided
at the selected operating point in simulation. Because the live
15-frame run rejected every candidate, Rsave should not be
interpreted as a measured hardware quantity.
The trade-off is asymmetric. A false rejection causes an
unnecessary exact recovery operation and therefore costs
latency or channel use. A false acceptance can silently deliver

The multiplicative confidence rule makes a low physical

C. Confidence Calibration as a Communication-Layer Func-
tion
The confidence threshold should not be viewed as a generic
machine-learning hyperparameter. In SemLiFi, it determines
a communication-layer routing decision. The calibration data
therefore need to represent the physical burst distribution and
the payload schemas for which the system will operate. If the
threshold is calibrated only on clean or synthetically corrupted
data, it may not represent the behavior of the receiver under
real optical occlusion.
The reported 664/641 burst split provides a basis for a
disjoint calibration/test design. The calibration set should be
used to select τ and the burst-penalty parameters, while the
test set should remain untouched until the final evaluation.
This separation is especially important when the objective is a
safety-oriented metric such as UFER. Threshold selection after
observing the test errors would produce an optimistic estimate.
A complete threshold study should report at least the accep-
tance rate, exact precision, FAR, FRR, UFER, retransmission-
avoidance rate, and recovery latency at each candidate threshold.
The present source reports the selected operating point but
not the complete sweep. Accordingly, the current paper treats
τ∗ = 0.80 as a reported operating point and identifies
the underlying sweep as a reproducibility item rather than
reconstructing missing measurements.
D. Task-Level Accuracy and Safety-Critical Fields
Exact reconstruction and task-level recovery answer different
questions. Exact reconstruction asks whether the missing
representation is identical to the transmitted representation.
Task-level accuracy asks whether a predefined field needed by
the application is correct. The reported BASR results contain a
substantial difference between these two metrics: exact accuracy
is 42.63±1.47% , whereas the evaluated motor-state field
reaches 100.0% accuracy.
This difference is useful only if the task field definition is
explicit. For example, a motor state encoded as MOTOR=ON
can be treated as correct when the recovered state is exactly
ON, even if another non-critical field differs. The 100.0% value
should therefore not be interpreted as 100.0% exact payload
recovery. The denominator and event definition used for this
metric must be retained with the raw evaluation data.
For safety-oriented telemetry, the safest policy may be to
define a field-specific acceptance rule. A reconstruction that
changes an actuation state could be forced to exact fallback
even when other fields have high confidence. The current CGFP
implementation provides a general structural-validity factor,
but the source does not report a completed field-specific risk
model. Such a model is a natural extension of the framework.
E. Latency Decomposition
The reported end-to-end latency can be understood as the
sum of communication, burst detection, inference, validation,
and fallback components. In the semantic-acceptance case, the
fallback term is absent; in the rejection case, the exact-recovery
term becomes dominant. This makes the system’s performance
dependent on the distribution of confidence outcomes rather
than only on the raw inference time.
The reported 2.79 ms mean BASR inference time is
therefore not directly comparable with the 790.7 ms end-to-
end continuous-burst latency. The former measures the model
computation on an edge CPU, while the latter includes the
full communication and recovery path. The difference in scale
also indicates why optimizing the physical and retransmission
components can be more important than shaving small amounts
from the neural inference time.
The live hardware latency range of 636.6–792.9 ms should
similarly be interpreted with its recovery behavior. Because
all 15 frames triggered fallback, the observed latency includes
exact-recovery actions rather than semantic-patch acceptance.
The result cannot establish what the latency would be for a
live frame that passes CGFP.
IX. EXPERIMENTALREPRODUCIBILITY ANDSTATISTICAL
RIGOR
A. Separation of Training, Calibration, and Testing
A defensible experimental pipeline should keep three kinds
of information separate. The BASR training data determine
model parameters. The CGFP calibration data determine the
threshold and confidence mapping. The final test data estimate
the reported performance. If bursts from the same physical event
are split across these sets, leakage can occur because adjacent
observations may share nearly identical optical conditions.
The supplied record explicitly describes the 664/641 CGFP
calibration/test split as disjoint at the event level. This is a
useful property. For the final paper, the authors should also
preserve the event identifier, physical profile, frame identifier,
payload instance, and random seed for each sample so that the
split can be audited.
B. Simulation Versus Hardware Evidence
The study combines physical measurements with simulation.
This is appropriate for a proof-of-concept system because the
hardware campaign establishes that the impairment exists and
the simulation permits larger controlled comparisons. However,
the two evidence types answer different questions.
The 56-event physical campaign measures the burst phe-
nomenon. The BASR and CGFP simulation evaluates the
recovery strategy over a larger set of derived conditions. The
15-frame hardware run tests the integrated decision behavior
under actual optical conditions. Because the hardware run
accepts no semantic patches, it cannot validate the simulation’s
retransmission-avoidance efficiency. This distinction should
remain explicit in every abstract, table, figure, and conclusion.

C. Meaning of Mean Plus or Minus Standard Deviation
The source reports 42.63±1.47% exact accuracy and
1.00±0.13% UFER across five simulation seeds. The reported
statistical treatment states that these seeds vary sampled frames
and burst events under a fixed model and pipeline configuration.
Thus, the standard deviation should not be described as
model-training uncertainty. To estimate training variability,
independent training runs with different initialization seeds
would be required.
Likewise, a five-seed simulation does not establish a con-
fidence interval for deployment performance. The variance is
useful as a sensitivity measure within the evaluated simulation
design, but the live hardware population is currently much
smaller. The paper therefore avoids attaching a population-
level statistical guarantee to the reported 15-frame hardware
result.
D. Avoiding Leakage Through Payload Templates
Structured telemetry can contain repeated templates. If the
same payload instance or nearly identical field sequence appears
in both training and testing, exact reconstruction accuracy may
be inflated because the model can exploit memorized structure.
A stronger future protocol should split by payload template or
device identifier in addition to splitting by burst event. This
would test whether BASR learns general structural relationships
rather than simply memorizing recurring messages.
E. Ablation Protocol Required for Causal Claims
The central novelty depends on the interaction between
burst awareness and confidence gating. A rigorous ablation
should therefore include at least the following configurations:
random-mask BASR without physical burst features; burst-
aware BASR without burst features in CGFP; CGFP without
the physical confidence factor; CGFP without the burst penalty;
CGFP without the syntax factor; and the full system. Each
configuration should be evaluated on the same held-out event
set.
The current source identifies this experiment as missing.
Consequently, the paper does not claim that every individual
factor is necessary based on the current measurements. Instead,
it reports the performance of the integrated configuration and
presents the ablation as a required next experiment.
F . Recommended Reporting Matrix
For each operating condition, a final dataset should contain
the physical burst profile, duration, estimated affected symbols,
frame length, BASR confidence, structural-validity result,
semantic decision, exact-fallback decision, final correctness,
task-field correctness, inference latency, and total recovery
latency. This event-level record would allow reviewers to
reproduce aggregate metrics and inspect false acceptances
directly.

X. ENGINEERINGDESIGNTRADEOFFS
A. ESP32 Versus More Powerful Edge Hardware
The ESP32 is appropriate for the proof-of-concept because
the transmitter and receiver require GPIO timing, ADC acquisi-
tion, Manchester decoding, burst detection, and communication
control in addition to the semantic inference path. A more
powerful processor could support larger models or higher
rates, but it would also make the prototype less representative
of a low-cost embedded edge implementation. The present
74,281-parameter model is intentionally constrained to keep
the inference component small.
This choice does not imply that every Transformer ar-
chitecture can run on an ESP32. The reported 2.79 ms
value is an edge CPU measurement associated with the
evaluated BASR implementation, and the complete deployment
footprint depends on the runtime, memory allocator, numerical
representation, and firmware integration. These details should
be measured explicitly before claiming microcontroller-level
deployment on a specific board.
B. Why Manchester Coding Is Retained
Manchester coding introduces transition redundancy and
therefore reduces raw payload efficiency compared with a
simple binary level scheme. It nevertheless provides a useful
timing structure for a small experimental testbed. The receiver
can use mid-bit sampling and transition checks to distinguish
valid symbols from ambiguous optical observations. Since
the study is focused on recovery logic rather than maximum
throughput, this trade-off is acceptable within the tested
configuration.
C. CRC and Semantic Recovery Serve Different Roles
CRC-8 validates bit-exact frames. BASR reconstructs miss-
ing content when bit-exact information is unavailable. These
functions should not be conflated. A CRC failure identifies
that the received frame is not an exact copy; it does not tell
the receiver what the missing value should be. Conversely, a
semantic candidate that passes a syntax check does not satisfy
the CRC of the original frame because the original missing
bits remain unknown.
The hybrid architecture therefore uses CRC and semantic
confidence at different stages. CRC is used for exact valida-
tion, while CGFP decides whether a semantic candidate is
--- PAGE 9 ---
9
sufficiently trustworthy to avoid exact recovery. When exact
fallback succeeds, the recovered frame can be validated with
CRC.
D. Why the Servo Flap Matters
A manually generated blockage can vary substantially
in duration, angle, and position. The servo flap provides
repeatability and allows the study to generate three broad
duration profiles. This does not make the resulting distribution
representative of every human or environmental obstruction, but
it provides a controlled experimental variable. Reproducibility
is especially important when comparing model training on
burst-aware masks against training on random masks.
E. Safety-Conservative Operating Point
The multiplicative confidence rule has a conservative in-
terpretation. If any factor is small, the final confidence is
reduced. A hard syntax failure sets the score to zero. The
system therefore prefers false rejection over false acceptance
when the confidence evidence is weak. This is appropriate
for the stated safety concern but can reduce the number of
accepted semantic patches.
The current live result illustrates this trade-off. Mean
confidence is approximately 0.46, well below the 0.80 threshold,
and all 15 frames fall back to exact recovery. The gate therefore
sacrifices retransmission avoidance to maintain conservative
behavior under those hardware conditions. Whether a different
threshold would provide a better operating point requires the
missing threshold-sweep data and a larger hardware test set.
XI. ALGORITHMICIMPLEMENTATION ANDOPERATING
ENVELOPE
A. Receiver-Side Procedure
The receiver-side procedure can be expressed as a sequence
of deterministic communication operations followed by con-
ditional semantic inference. First, the ESP32 acquires the
optical waveform and applies the calibrated threshold. Second,
Manchester symbols are decoded and the frame delimiter,
length, sequence identifier, and CRC fields are parsed. Third,
a contiguous unavailable region is identified from missing
symbols, transition violations, or frame-level integrity failure.
Fourth, the receiver estimates the burst duration and affected-
symbol count and extracts the surviving context.
The semantic stage is entered only after the physical
processing has established a candidate loss interval. BASR
receives the surviving prefix, suffix, burst metadata, and payload
structure and returns a candidate missing segment with token-
level confidence. CGFP then evaluates structural validity and
computes the combined score. If the score is below the
threshold, the receiver does not deliver the semantic candidate.
Instead, it requests exact recovery. The final payload is then
subjected to the exact integrity mechanism available to the
receiver.

clean frame bypasses the semantic model. Semantic inference

This reduces unnecessary inference and prevents the model
from modifying error-free traffic.

XIII. DISCUSSION
A. Semantic Usefulness Versus Exact Correctness
The BASR result demonstrates why semantic usefulness
and exact correctness should be reported separately. An exact
reconstruction accuracy of 42.63% would be insufficient if
the objective were bit-perfect replacement of every missing
--- PAGE 12 ---
12
segment. The reported 100.0% motor-state accuracy, however,
indicates that the evaluated application field remained correct
despite imperfect character-level reconstruction. This does not
make the semantic output safe by itself; an incorrect value in
another field can still be operationally harmful. CGFP therefore
provides the missing decision layer.
B. Why Burst Awareness Matters
The physical impairment is not an arbitrary mask. It is a
contiguous loss interval produced by an obstruction. Burst
duration determines how much context survives and how much
of the payload is unavailable. Including burst metadata in
BASR and in the confidence calculation therefore connects the
physical and semantic layers. The approach is intended to be
more physically grounded than training solely on independent
random token masks.
C. Why Exact Fallback Remains Necessary
The central design principle is that semantic reconstruction
is an optional shortcut to exact recovery, not a replacement
for exact recovery. If the semantic candidate is sufficiently
trustworthy under the calibrated criterion, the system can avoid
retransmission. If confidence is low or structural validation
fails, exact recovery remains available. This architecture
is particularly relevant when the cost of a false semantic
acceptance is larger than the cost of a false rejection.
D. Interpretation of the Latency Result
The approximately 49.3% reduction relative to the reported
ARQ latency is meaningful only under the continuous-burst
simulation conditions stated in the source. The result does
not establish a universal latency advantage because latency
depends on burst duration, retransmission protocol, frame
length, processing hardware, and the availability of the return
channel. The 2.79 ms BASR inference time is comparatively
small relative to the reported end-to-end latency, but it is not
the only computation or communication cost.
E. Practical Deployment Considerations
A physical deployment would need to account for ambient
illumination, optical alignment, dimming behavior, LED driver
dynamics, receiver saturation, and mobility. The approximate
50-count receiver threshold was calibrated under the reported
laboratory conditions. A moving receiver or a change in ambient
light could shift the illuminated and dark distributions and
therefore alter the effective confidence term.
The 1000 bps demonstration rate also limits the scope of
the current evaluation. A higher-rate implementation could
change the relationship between physical burst duration and
affected bytes. In particular, at a higher rate the same physical
obstruction could erase more symbols, increasing the burden
on both BASR and exact fallback.
F . Energy Considerations
The source does not report direct energy measurements.
It is reasonable as an engineering hypothesis that avoiding
retransmissions can reduce repeated transmitter and receiver
activity, but this has not been quantified in the study. Therefore,
energy savings are treated as a future measurement rather than
a demonstrated result.
G. Broader Optical Applicability
The architectural pattern could be investigated for other
optical channels experiencing contiguous loss, including free-
space and underwater optical links. However, the burst distri-
bution, physical confidence calibration, and structural payload
constraints would have to be re-characterized for each channel.
The current results should not be transferred directly to those
settings.
XIV. LIMITATIONS ANDTHREATS TOVALIDITY
A. Physical Realism of Occlusion
The servo flap provides repeatable blockage but cannot
reproduce every real obstruction. Human motion, partial opacity,
object shape, angle of incidence, receiver orientation, and
ambient illumination can produce different burst statistics. The
three measured profiles are therefore a controlled starting point
rather than an exhaustive model of physical LiFi blockage.
B. Payload Generality
BASR depends on structured or partially predictable teleme-
try. It should not be generalized to arbitrary encrypted or
structureless binary data. Different payload schemas may
require different model training and structural-validity rules.
C. Live-Hardware Sample Size
The live experiment contains only 15 frames. More im-
portantly, every frame was rejected by CGFP. The live run
therefore supports the rejection/fallback safety behavior but
does not establish a hardware patch-acceptance rate or hardware
retransmission-avoidance efficiency.
D. Missing Component Ablations
The available experimental record does not contain com-
pleted ablation measurements separating burst-aware training
from random masking or isolating the contribution of individual
CGFP factors. As a result, the current data cannot prove that
each component independently causes the reported end-to-end
improvement. This should be addressed before making stronger
causal claims.
E. Threshold Calibration
The reported threshold τ∗ = 0.80 and the burst-penalty
parameters are specific to the evaluated telemetry and burst
distribution. The complete threshold sweep is not included
in the supplied record. The operating point should therefore
be reproduced from the original calibration logs before final
submission.

 XIV. LIMITATIONS AND THREATS TO VALIDITY
A. Physical Realism of Occlusion
The servo flap provides repeatable blockage but cannot reproduce every
real obstruction. Human motion, partial opacity, object shape, angle of
incidence, receiver orientation, and ambient illumination can produce
different burst statistics. The three measured profiles should therefore be
interpreted as a controlled experimental starting point rather than an
exhaustive model of physical LiFi blockage. The controlled actuator is
valuable for repeatability, but it does not establish that the measured
distribution is representative of all indoor optical environments.
B. Payload Generality
BASR depends on structured or partially predictable telemetry. The
evaluated examples contain explicit field names, delimiters, numeric
ranges, and state labels. These properties provide contextual constraints
that are not available for arbitrary encrypted or structureless binary
payloads. Different schemas would require their own training
distribution, parsing rules, and structural-validity constraints. The
reported accuracy should therefore be scoped to the structured-telemetry
setting used in the study.
C. Live-Hardware Sample Size
The live experiment contains only 15 frames, and every low-confidence
reconstruction was rejected by CGFP. The experiment therefore provides
evidence about conservative fallback behavior rather than about the
hardware acceptance rate of semantic patches. In particular, the reported
27.15% retransmission avoidance remains a simulation result and should
not be presented as a measured hardware efficiency.
D. Missing Component Ablations
The supplied record does not contain completed ablation measurements
that separately isolate burst-aware training, the physical-confidence
factor, the burst penalty, and the structural-validity term. Consequently,
the current evidence supports the integrated system result but does not
establish that each individual component is necessary for the observed
performance. A controlled ablation on the same held-out event set is
required before stronger causal claims are made.
E. Threshold Calibration
The reported operating threshold is τ* = 0.80, with burst-penalty
parameters α = 0.50 and γ = 1.15. The complete threshold sweep is not
included in the supplied record. Threshold selection should therefore be
reproduced from the original calibration logs, and the final paper should
report acceptance rate, precision, FAR, FRR, UFER, retransmission
avoidance, and recovery latency across the candidate thresholds.
F. UFER Acceptance Bound
The manuscript describes a 2% UFER acceptance bound, but the
supplied record does not include the preregistration document. The status
of that bound should be independently verified before submission. If no
formal preregistration exists, the wording should identify the criterion as
an evaluation threshold rather than a preregistered commitment.
G. Bibliographic and Author Verification
The final submission should preserve the verified conference-paper
metadata for the Nennouche et al. citation [11] and should be checked by
all authors for author order, affiliations, and institutional e-mail
addresses. These checks are editorial requirements and are not
experimental evidence.

precision and patch error. The experiment should preserve event-level
identifiers so that physical conditions can be separated from repeated
payload templates.
B. Controlled Ablation Study
BASR should be trained and evaluated under at least two masking
regimes: independent random masking and masks sampled from the
measured optical burst distribution. The CGFP study should then remove
one confidence factor at a time while keeping the same test events. This
design can distinguish the effect of physical burst awareness from the
effect of confidence gating and structural validation.
C. Threshold-Sweep Publication
A complete threshold study should report acceptance rate, exact
precision, FAR, FRR, UFER, retransmission avoidance, and latency over
a dense candidate set. The selected τ* = 0.80 can then be justified using a
criterion defined before the final test evaluation. Reporting the complete
curve is particularly important because a single operating point does not
reveal the reliability–latency trade-off.
D. Higher-Rate Evaluation
The prototype uses a 1000 bps raw signaling rate. Increasing the rate
changes the number of symbols affected by a fixed physical obstruction
duration. The resulting interaction among burst length, surviving context,
BASR accuracy, inference time, and retransmission cost should therefore
be measured directly rather than extrapolated from the current operating
point.
E. Broader Physical Conditions
Future physical experiments should vary obstruction opacity, size, angle,
speed, and receiver orientation. Ambient-light conditions and
source–receiver separation should also be varied independently. Such
measurements would provide a broader empirical basis for calibrating
Cphys and Pburst. Cross-domain evaluation in free-space or underwater
optical links would require new burst characterization and
payload-specific calibration.
F. Security-Oriented Extensions
If the architecture is considered for safety-relevant deployments,
deliberate telemetry spoofing, syntactically valid false data, adversarial
burst patterns, and optical manipulation should be evaluated separately.
These cases are outside the current non-adversarial obstruction threat
model and should not be conflated with the reported UFER
measurements.
 XVII. ETHICAL AND SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS
A. Semantic Substitution Risk
The principal safety concern is silent semantic substitution: a
reconstructed value may be plausible and syntactically valid while
differing from the transmitted value. Semantic confidence is therefore
treated as a routing signal rather than as proof of correctness. The
structural gate and exact fallback are designed to reduce the chance that
an uncertain candidate is delivered as if it were exact.
B. Scope of the Safety Evidence
The current study does not evaluate deliberate frame forgery, malicious
telemetry injection, adversarial examples, or intentional optical
manipulation. The reported UFER should therefore be interpreted only
within the tested physical-burst and structured-telemetry setting. The live
experiment produced 0.00% UFER for 15 frames, but the small sample
does not establish a population-level security or reliability guarantee.
 XVIII. EXTENDED QUANTITATIVE ANALYSIS
A. Coding Capability Versus Observed Burst Length
The selected RS(33,25) configuration contains eight parity symbols and
has a nominal correction capability of t = 4 symbol errors under the
evaluated configuration. The physical campaign reports approximately
10–25 contiguous corrupted bytes during obstruction. This difference
explains why a finite correction window can be exceeded in some events,
although the exact mapping from corrupted bytes to decoder symbols
depends on frame boundaries, Manchester coding, and the decoder
treatment of erasures and errors. The reported 37.5% recovery rate is
therefore an empirical result for the implemented configuration rather
than a theoretical limit on Reed–Solomon coding.
B. Retransmission Avoidance
For N affected frames, if M frames still require exact recovery, the
retransmission-saving fraction is Rsave = 1 − M/N. At the reported
operating point, the simulation avoids 27.15% of retransmissions.
Because every one of the 15 live frames invokes fallback, no hardware
retransmission-saving percentage is established by the current
experiment.
C. Task-Level Reliability
The difference between exact reconstruction and task-level correctness is
not only a reporting distinction; it changes the acceptance policy. A field
that controls actuation or a safety-relevant state can be assigned a stricter
rule than a non-critical descriptive field. Under the current architecture,
such a policy can be implemented by extending the structural-validity
factor so that selected fields force exact fallback whenever their
reconstructed value is uncertain.
D. Confidence Factor Interaction
The multiplicative CGFP score makes the decision sensitive to each
factor. A low physical-confidence estimate, a severe burst penalty, or a
low token-level confidence can reduce the combined score below τ*. The
hard syntax factor is stronger still: Ssyntax = 0 forces fallback regardless
of learned confidence. This design reflects the asymmetric cost of false
acceptance versus false rejection.
E. Mixed-Burst Operating Point
Under the reported mixed condition in which 40% of frames experience
bursts, SemLiFi achieves 100% delivery with a latency range of
592.5–595.5 ms. The supplied record does not provide a comparable
UFER value for this condition. The result therefore illustrates the
dependence of total recovery cost on burst prevalence without
establishing a continuous latency curve.

C. Latency Decomposition
The total recovery time can be decomposed as Ttotal = Tcommunication +
Tdetection + Tinference + Tfallback. The reported BASR inference time is
2.79 ms, whereas the continuous-burst end-to-end SemLiFi latency is
790.7 ms. These quantities measure different stages and should not be
compared as if they were equivalent. The large difference indicates that
retransmission and communication components dominate the end-to-end
timing under severe burst conditions.

D. Relative Latency Reduction
Using the reported continuous-burst values, the relative reduction with
respect to the Stop-and-Wait ARQ condition is 1 − 790.7/1560.0 ≈
49.3%. This calculation is a derived comparison of the reported
operating point. It does not establish a universal latency advantage
because the result depends on burst prevalence, frame length,
return-channel behavior, retransmission policy, and processing hardware.
E. BASR Exact and Task-Level Accuracy
The reported exact reconstruction accuracy is 42.63 ± 1.47%, while the
evaluated motor-state field reaches 100.0% accuracy. The distinction is
central: exact reconstruction requires the missing segment to match the
transmitted representation, whereas task-level accuracy asks whether a
predefined application field is correct. The latter can remain correct even
when other non-critical characters differ. The denominator and event
definition for the motor-state metric should be retained with the raw
evaluation data.
 Fig. 7. Reported BASR exact reconstruction and motor-state field accuracy. The

F. Model Size and Edge Constraints
The BASR model contains 74,281 trainable parameters against a
75,000-parameter budget, corresponding to 99.04% utilization. A simple
32-bit storage calculation gives approximately 290 KB for the weights,
while idealized 8-bit storage gives approximately 73 KB. These are
storage estimates, not measurements of the complete runtime footprint.
The reported 2.79 ms inference time is an edge-CPU measurement and
should not be interpreted as proof that the entire Transformer runtime fits
within an ESP32 memory budget without additional deployment
measurements.
G. CGFP Calibration/Test Separation
The reported calibration and test sets contain 664 and 641 bursts,
respectively, and are described as disjoint at the event level. This
separation is important because threshold selection must not use the final
test outcomes. The split provides a clear basis for auditing whether the
calibrated threshold was selected independently of the final performance
estimate.
 Fig. 8. Reported event-level separation of the CGFP calibration and test sets.
H. Threshold Interpretation
The selected threshold should be interpreted as a communication-layer
routing point rather than as a generic machine-learning hyperparameter.
A higher threshold would generally be expected to reduce semantic
acceptance while increasing exact fallback, whereas a lower threshold
would admit more candidates and require careful monitoring of false
acceptance. Because the complete sweep is not supplied, these
directional effects are design expectations rather than measured curves.
I. Evidence Boundary
The continuous-burst simulation and the live hardware run answer
different questions. The simulation provides the reported latency, UFER,
and retransmission-avoidance measurements under controlled seeds and
burst conditions. The hardware run establishes that the physical receiver,
burst detector, BASR path, and fallback gate can operate together, but its
15-frame size and universal fallback behavior do not support a hardware
estimate of semantic acceptance efficiency.

K. Interpretation of the Evidence
The quantitative record supports three distinct observations: the optical

 XIX. CONCLUSION
This paper presented SemLiFi, a cross-layer framework for burst
recovery on physically obstructed LiFi links. The architecture combines
an ESP32-based optical testbed, controlled servo-driven occlusion,
empirical burst characterization, a compact Burst-Aware Semantic
Reconstruction model, and a Confidence-Gated Fallback Protocol. The
central design principle is to treat semantic reconstruction as a
conditional optimization of exact recovery rather than as a replacement
for exact recovery.
The reported clean-link experiment achieves 449.0 ms mean latency with
zero bit errors and 100% CRC validation. The physical occlusion
campaign contains 56 events and produces contiguous losses that can
exceed the correction capability of the evaluated RS(33,25)
configuration. BASR contains 74,281 parameters, reports 42.63 ± 1.47%
exact reconstruction accuracy, 100.0% accuracy on the evaluated
motor-state field, and 2.79 ms mean edge-CPU inference latency. At τ* =
0.80, the reported simulation avoids 27.15% of retransmissions, with
97.13% accepted-patch precision and a 0.78% patch-error rate.
Under continuous-burst simulation, SemLiFi reports 100% delivery at
790.7 ms and 1.00 ± 0.13% UFER, compared with 1560.0 ms for the
reported Stop-and-Wait ARQ condition. The 15-frame live hardware
experiment reaches 100% delivery and 0.00% UFER, but every semantic
reconstruction is rejected. Thus, the live experiment provides evidence
for conservative fallback behavior rather than hardware
semantic-patching efficiency. Larger physical testing, complete
threshold sweeps, component ablations, and broader obstruction
conditions remain necessary before the simulation-level efficiency can
be generalized to practical LiFi deployments.

 INTERPRETATION OF THE FINAL RESULTS
The principal contribution of the reported study is the integration of
physical burst characterization, contextual reconstruction, and
confidence-gated routing in a single recovery pipeline. The architecture
does not require semantic reconstruction to succeed on every burst.
Instead, it treats the semantic path as conditional: a candidate is useful
only when its confidence and structural validity are sufficient; otherwise,
exact recovery is requested. This operating principle explains why the
live experiment can report zero undetected frame errors even though no
semantic patch is accepted.
For journal review, the distinction between feasibility and generalization
is important. The physical testbed demonstrates that the targeted
impairment can be generated and measured repeatably. The simulation
provides the larger quantitative evaluation of BASR and CGFP. The
hardware experiment demonstrates integrated fallback behavior. These
three evidence layers complement one another, but they should not be
merged into a single claim of deployment-level performance.
The final manuscript should therefore retain the reported numerical
values exactly, label derived quantities as calculations, and preserve the
stated limitations. In particular, the small hardware sample, missing
threshold sweep, incomplete ablation evidence, and controlled nature of
the obstruction campaign should remain visible to reviewers. This
presentation provides a clearer and more defensible account of what the
current experimental record establishes.
